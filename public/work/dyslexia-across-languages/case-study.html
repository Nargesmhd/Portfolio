Dyslexia doesn't switch languages the way your keyboard does

A UX research case study. Secondary research phase, built on peer-reviewed sources, with an interview study designed next.

The problem

Most dyslexia-friendly features in software were designed around English. Dyslexic users who live in two languages get half a solution.

Dyslexia shows up differently in each writing system. In English it looks like spelling and decoding errors. In Farsi, everyday text drops vowel marks, so reading means sounding out consonants. In Mandarin there is no alphabet: typing means picking the right character from a list of look-alikes.

A bilingual dyslexic person switches between these systems dozens of times a day. Every switch resets the keyboard, the autocorrect and the predictions. Nobody owns that moment.

Research question

When bilingual adults with dyslexia switch languages on their phone or computer, where do things break, and what workarounds have they built?

Scope

  • Languages: English paired with Farsi and with Mandarin. Chosen because the three scripts fail in different ways.
  • Users: adults who use both languages on a device most days. Self-identified dyslexia counts.
  • Moments: messaging, search, forms, reading. The focus is the switch, not dyslexia in general.

Why this question: it names one observable moment, and "workarounds" turns every user story into a design opportunity.

What I did

This phase is secondary research built on peer-reviewed studies only. I had no access to participants, so I worked from published evidence and was explicit about where it stops.

  1. Reviewed the literature on dyslexia in Persian and Chinese scripts, including brain-imaging work showing the two scripts use different reading pathways, and studies of adults with dyslexia reading, spelling and typing in a second language.
  2. Mapped what each study covers and what it does not. Almost all of it is lab reading and spelling tasks, mostly with children. None covers bilingual adults switching languages on a device.
  3. Built three provisional personas, each a pattern rather than a person. Every trait is tagged S (supported by a peer-reviewed source) or H (hypothesis to test).
  4. Extracted findings and recommendations, with confidence rated low until interviews confirm them.

Full personas, source list and tags are in Personas and sources.

What I found

Four findings. All are low confidence until interviews confirm them; that is stated, not hidden.

1. Dyslexia does not transfer between scripts. Brain-imaging research shows Chinese and alphabetic reading impairments involve different regions, and early bilingual reading changes how dyslexia shows up in adults. Products still treat "dyslexic user" as one account-level setting.

2. The switch moment is unowned. Dyslexic spelling errors differ by language, yet no study looks at what happens when a user switches languages on a device. The hypothesis: keyboard, autocorrect and predictions each guess separately, and dyslexic users pay more for each wrong guess.

3. Each script breaks at a different step. Farsi: decoding without vowel marks. English: spelling. Mandarin: choosing between look-alike characters. One "dyslexia mode" cannot fix all three.

4. Nobody has asked these users about their phones. Published research covers reading and spelling in lab tasks, mostly with children. Adults with dyslexia are known to type more slowly and less accurately, but no peer-reviewed study covers bilingual adults on their devices. That gap is the project's main contribution.

Try it

  • Feel the switch — three two-minute trials: read Farsi without vowels, pick between look-alike Chinese characters, and type a mixed-language text while autocorrect fights you.
  • Friction map — one message from first tap to send, with nine stops, each tagged by persona, evidence and fix to test.

What I would do next

A six-to-eight person interview study, two to three per language pair, using the H hypotheses as the interview guide.

  • Format: 45-minute remote sessions, with two or three run as screen-shares during a real messaging task.
  • Pre-task: each participant screenshots three moments in a week where a language switch tripped them up. The interview starts from real artifacts, not memory.
  • Recruitment: dyslexia communities online, Iranian and Chinese community groups in the Toronto area, university accessibility offices, snowball referrals.
  • Questions to settle: does dyslexia make switching worse than it is for any bilingual user? Which workarounds are real? Is the character picker the true pain point?

Output: validated personas, a friction map of the switch moment, and prioritized recommendations.

What this project shows about how I work

  • I separate evidence from assumption. Every persona trait is tagged sourced or hypothesis. Nothing invented is presented as data.
  • I scope tightly. One moment (the switch), three scripts, four findings. Depth over breadth.
  • I find the gap. The most useful output is not a recommendation; it is spotting that no one has studied adult bilingual dyslexic users on their devices.
  • I know what comes next. The hypotheses are already an interview guide.

My background is in design, support and teaching. All three taught me the same thing: people rarely tell you where they are stuck, but they always show you through the workarounds they invent. This project is built on looking for those workarounds.
